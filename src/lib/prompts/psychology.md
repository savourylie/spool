# Social Media Psychology Reference

Reference for LLM-powered features that judge or generate Threads
posts. Organized by the four decisions a post has to win: does it
**hook** attention, does it drive **sharing**, does it **persuade**, and
which **processing route** the reader will use. Cite frameworks by
name (STEPPS, ELM, Cialdini) so downstream prompts and reviews can
cross-reference.

Every mechanism here is a reader-centric lens: what does the reader
feel, and what do they do next. None of these are license to
manipulate — they are the vocabulary for a post that earns attention
honestly.

## Part 1 — Hook Triggers

What makes the first 1–2 lines stop the scroll.

### Information Gap (Loewenstein, 1994)

Curiosity is the gap between *what I know* and *what I want to know*.
The curve is an inverted U: readers need just enough priming to notice
the gap. Full novelty reads as irrelevant; full familiarity reads as
boring. Middle ground ("I kind of know this, but not the part you
just named") produces the strongest pull.

*Application:* the hook should surface a gap the target reader didn't
realize they had, not a gap they're already tired of. Importance of
the gap drives how hard they work to close it.

### Zeigarnik Effect / Open Loops

Unfinished tasks occupy more mental space than finished ones. A post
that opens a loop — question raised, tension set up, conclusion
deferred — holds attention longer. Series content uses the same
mechanism at the post level.

*Application:* every open loop needs a clean closing. Too many
unresolved loops in a single post turns curiosity into frustration.

### Peak-End Rule (Kahneman & Fredrickson)

Readers remember the emotional peak and the ending, not the middle.
Length barely affects how people describe a post afterward; the peak
line and the close do almost all the work.

*Application:* each post needs one line that hits hardest, and a close
that lands — not a close that trails off into a generic wrap-up.

### Hook / Payoff Gap

The most dangerous shape isn't "weak hook." It's "strong hook, weak
payoff." High expectation + low delivery produces short dwell,
negative feedback, and lost trust. A medium hook with strong payoff
rarely breaks out, but it compounds; the reverse does not.

*Application:* check whether the body starts delivering in the first
third, not the last line. A reader who leaves at 30% should already
have taken something away.

## Part 2 — Share Motives (STEPPS — Jonah Berger)

Six forces that make content travel. The check question after each
one is what to ask of a draft.

### Social Currency

Does sharing this make the sharer look smart, early, or inside?
"Insider-only" information is strong social currency; generic tips
are not. *Check:* if someone forwarded this, what does it say about
them?

### Triggers

What everyday moment reminds someone of this post? "Friday" is a
stronger trigger than "holidays" because it recurs weekly. *Check:*
what normal event in the reader's week would make this post come back
to mind?

### Emotion

The driver is *arousal*, not valence. Awe, anger, anxiety, excitement
— all high-arousal — promote sharing. Sadness and contentment don't.
Negative content spreads on arousal, not because negativity is the
goal. *Check:* does the reader finish the post more activated than
they started, in either direction?

### Public

Behaviors that are visible get copied. Social-share buttons are
public; DM sends are private but still outsized. *Check:* if someone
quotes this post in their own feed, does the quote hold up without
context?

### Practical Value

Does this save someone time, help them avoid a mistake, or teach
them a move they can use this week? Packaged knowledge travels; vague
advice doesn't. *Check:* can the reader walk away with one move they
can apply today?

### Stories

Information rides inside narrative. The test is whether the story and
the message are inseparable — if the story could be removed and the
message still stands, the story isn't carrying any weight. *Check:*
would the reader retell this as a story, or as a claim?

## Part 3 — Persuasion (Cialdini's 7)

The classical persuasion levers, translated for social content.

### Reciprocity

When a creator gives concrete value upfront — a framework, a tactic,
a clean answer — readers feel a pull to return the favor. Real value
is the condition; charity vibes backfire. *Application:* start with
give, not ask.

### Commitment & Consistency

A small public act — voting in a poll, replying with a short opinion
— raises the chance of a larger one later. Readers defend what they
said they believed. *Application:* a lightweight first action ("which
of these two does your team actually do?") beats a cold ask for a
share.

### Social Proof

Under uncertainty, people copy others. Comment count, visible
endorsements, and "most people I talk to ask this" framing work as
proof. UGC outweighs branded claims. *Application:* show the proof
without faking it; invented numbers unwind fast.

### Authority

Competence signals persuade, but the signal doesn't have to be titles.
Real test data, before/after artifacts, named failures, and
domain-specific detail beat credential-dropping. *Application:*
demonstrate knowledge through specificity; don't announce it.

### Liking

Readers accept persuasion from people they like. Likeability comes
from similarity, self-disclosure, warmth, and repeated exposure. A
personal voice outperforms an institutional one in a feed. *Application:*
the post should sound like a person with a specific history, not a
house style.

### Scarcity

Rare information, narrow time windows, and first-hand access all
raise perceived value. Scarcity paired with loss framing ("you'll
miss this") doubles down, but only when it's real. *Application:* if
the scarcity is manufactured, readers spot it and punish the account
with negative feedback.

### Unity (added 2016)

Stronger than Liking: the sense that *we are the same kind of
people*. Insider vocabulary, shared enemies (bad practices, stale
ideas), and "we" framing build in-group belonging. *Application:* the
post should make the target reader feel they're inside, not being
pitched to.

## Part 4 — Processing Routes (Elaboration Likelihood Model)

Petty & Cacioppo (1986). Two paths the reader might take through your
post; the path is chosen by how motivated and able they are to
elaborate.

- **Central route.** High motivation + high cognitive bandwidth. The
  reader actually weighs the argument. Attitude changes via central
  route stick and predict future behavior.
- **Peripheral route.** Low motivation or low bandwidth. The reader
  leans on peripheral cues — visible engagement, author cues, vivid
  framing, tidy structure. Attitude changes via peripheral route are
  shallow and reversible.

A feed is a peripheral-route environment by default: competing
signals, quick scrolls, divided attention. But once the reader
elaborates, central wins.

**Dual-path practical strategy:**

- Opening runs on peripheral cues — pattern interruption, a concrete
  number, a named stance — to win the first 2–3 seconds.
- Body switches to central — real argument, real evidence — while the
  reader is paying attention.
- Close uses peripheral again — one sharp takeaway, one invitation to
  act.

The goal is to create a high-elaboration window inside a low-
elaboration environment.

## Part 5 — Quick Reference

| Theory | One-liner | Where it applies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Information Gap | Curiosity peaks at medium knowledge | Hook |
| Zeigarnik Effect | Unfinished tasks hold attention | Hook, series content |
| Peak-End Rule | Readers only remember peak + ending | Emotional arc, close |
| Hook/Payoff Gap | Strong hook + weak body is the worst shape | Hook-body alignment |
| STEPPS (Berger) | Six forces that drive sharing | Share-motive check |
| Cialdini's 7 | Reciprocity / Commitment / Social Proof / Authority / Liking / Scarcity / Unity | Persuasion levers, CTA |
| ELM (Petty & Cacioppo) | High-elaboration = central, low = peripheral | Opening vs body vs close |
| Endowment / IKEA | Readers value what they contributed to | Comment-thread design |
| Negativity Bias | Negative signals hit harder than positive ones | Open frame, avoid rage-bait |
| Dunbar's Number | Social bandwidth caps near 150 | Community sizing |
| Pratfall Effect | Small visible mistakes raise likeability | Self-disclosure |
| Social Identity Theory | Group membership shapes self-concept | Unity / in-group framing |
| Self-Determination | Autonomy, competence, relatedness drive intrinsic motivation | Long-term engagement design |

Rule of use: cite the lens when you name a finding, keep the
one-liner in mind when you apply it, and never stack three frameworks
in one paragraph — that's an AI-detection marker (see `ai-detection.md`).
